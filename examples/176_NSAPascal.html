Pascal's theorem and its degenerate cases

Both widgets construct, with joins and meets only, the hexagon ABCDEF inscribed in the circle k, the intersections X, Y and Z of its opposite sides, and the line p through X and Y. By Pascal's theorem, Z lies on p as well.

Classical geometry gets a family of further theorems out of it by letting neighbouring vertices coincide: the side through them becomes the tangent there. With B on A, AB is the tangent at A (Pascal for pentagons); with B on A and D on C, two tangents (quadrilaterals); with B on A, D on C and F on E, the tangents of a triangle meet the opposite sides in three points on a line. These are the cases that give ruler-only constructions of tangents to a conic.

Use the buttons, or drag vertices onto each other (a vertex dropped next to another one snaps onto it). On the left, the sides through coinciding vertices are undefined, or rounding noise if the vertices coincide up to rounding only, and so are X, Y and Z, or they are no longer collinear. On the right, with nsa: true (see 173_NSA.html), each such side is evaluated with infinitesimally separated vertices. Its limit, the tangent, does not depend on how they are separated, so it is shown, and the degenerate theorems hold. Move O or the remaining vertices to see that the tangents stay tangents.
